Slide 1 - Fintech Marketing Intelligence
Good [morning/afternoon]. Our project is called Fintech Marketing Intelligence: Complaint Signal Analysis and Prescriptive Strategy. We analyzed customer complaint behavior, especially CFPB complaint data, to understand where financial customers escalate, what patterns predict escalation, and what product opportunities those signals point toward for fintech companies.

Slide 2 - Agenda
Today we will move through the project in a simple sequence. First, we introduce the data set and our main research question. Then we explain the cleaning process, walk through five analysis questions, summarize the escalation prediction model, and end with strategic recommendations and key learnings.

Slide 3 - The Data Set
The data set is our foundation. It combines customer behavior records across sources, but for the core analysis we focus on CFPB complaint rows because they give us structured fields like product, company, issue type, customer action, and complaint text. That makes the data useful for both descriptive analysis and predictive modeling.

Slide 4 - About the Data
This project uses `master_customer_behavior_clean.csv`. The key fields include company, product or service, complaint text, issue, sentiment labels, customer action, and source metadata. The most important analytical lens is complaint-channel intelligence. This means we are not claiming that the data represents all financial customers. We are studying the customers who entered a complaint pathway and using that as a signal of friction, risk, and unmet needs.

Slide 5 - Shape and Size
The cleaned data has 777,867 total rows and 17 columns. Our CFPB core analysis subset includes 755,671 labeled rows, concentrated mainly from 2014 to 2017. We also created fintech proxy cohorts. PayPal and Venmo have 3,655 rows, Square and Block-side rows have 687 rows, and the large-bank baseline has 167,474 rows. The large-bank baseline helps us avoid treating bank-heavy complaint patterns as if they automatically apply to fintech.

Slide 6 - Missing Values
The missing values are concentrated in URL, issue, and country. URL is missing for 2,702 rows, but source links are not used as model features. Issue is missing for 22,196 rows, mainly outside the CFPB rows, and all CFPB rows used in the core analysis are complete on this field. Country is missing for 26,937 rows, but geography is not part of the model. So the missingness does not block the central analysis.

Slide 7 - Driving Question
This section introduces the question that connects the whole project. Instead of only asking what customers complain about, we ask what complaint patterns reveal about escalation risk and product opportunity.

Slide 8 - Driving Question Text
Our driving question is: How do CFPB complaint patterns, including issue type, product line, company, and customer action, signal escalation risk, and which startup and enterprise opportunities does that signal point to? This framing matters because it connects analytics to action. We are not just counting complaints; we are using the patterns to prioritize what companies should build or fix.

Slide 9 - How Does Our Analysis Answer It?
We answer the question in four layers. The descriptive layer maps complaint volume, escalation rates, and issue patterns by product and company tier. The predictive layer uses complaint text and product type to predict formal escalation. The prescriptive layer translates the signal clusters into product recommendations, such as billing clarity, dispute handling, lifecycle guardrails, and fintech-specific workflows. Finally, the strategic layer identifies bigger market gaps where new products could exist, such as complaint intelligence, trust scoring, and prevention tools.

Slide 10 - Data Cleaning
Before analysis, we standardized the raw data. The goal was not to over-clean the file, but to make the fields reliable enough for repeatable descriptive tables, modeling, and dashboard use.

Slide 11 - Step-by-Step Process
The cleaning process had seven main steps. We dropped the rating column because it was not central to the analysis. We defined which columns were allowed to be empty, such as URL and country, then removed rows missing required analytical fields. We parsed the issue field from raw metadata, created `sentiment_revised_label` so formal CFPB complaints were treated as negative for comparison, created `trends_keyword` for Google Trends rows, and exported the standardized clean file.

Slide 12 - Data Analysis: Five Questions
The analysis section is organized around five questions. These questions move from what is happening, to where it is happening, to whether we can predict escalation, and finally whether the signal is strong enough to support product prioritization.

Slide 13 - Question 1: Product and Issue Complaint Volume
For question one, we counted complaints by product type and issue category, then ranked them by volume using the CFPB-only subset. The highest-volume areas were mortgage, debt collection, bank account or service, and credit reporting. Dominant issues included billing disputes, collection attempts on debts not owed, and servicing failures. The takeaway is that complaint volume is not evenly distributed. It clusters around a few product and issue combinations, which means prioritization should start there.

Slide 14 - Visual Evidence for Question 1
This visual supports the first analysis question. The main point to emphasize is concentration. A small number of product and issue categories carry a large share of the complaint burden. That concentration is useful because it tells product teams where fixes are likely to have the largest measurable impact.

Slide 15 - Question 2: Formal Complaint Distribution
For question two, we looked at customer action types and calculated how often CFPB rows were formal complaints versus other outcomes. The formal complaint share is very high at about 97.8 percent, with only about 2.2 percent falling into in-progress, closed-without-relief, or similar categories. This matters because the CFPB slice is already an escalation-heavy channel. So we treat it as a risk and complaint-intelligence lens, not as a general customer satisfaction survey.

Slide 16 - Question 3: Major Players vs Smaller Players
For question three, we compared company profiles after filtering out companies with too few rows for reliable patterns. Major players tend to have more concentrated complaint profiles because they have more volume. Smaller fintech-related cohorts are noisier, but they show distinct patterns. PayPal and Venmo are more concentrated around money movement, fraud or scam concerns, transaction issues, and funds availability. Square and Block-side rows are more debt-collection heavy in this extract. This is why the fintech recommendation should not simply copy a mortgage-heavy large-bank strategy.

Slide 17 - Visual Evidence for Question 3
This visual is about competitive contrast. Large banks and fintech proxy cohorts fail in different zones. Large banks show more mortgage, servicing, and account-heavy patterns, while fintech cohorts show payment reliability, fraud handling, transaction clarity, and collections transparency issues. That contrast supports tailoring recommendations by company type instead of using one generic financial-services playbook.

Slide 18 - Question 4: Predicting Escalation
For question four, we built a logistic regression classifier using complaint text and product line. The model trained on 2014 to 2015 data and tested on held-back 2016 to 2017 data. The key finding is that escalation is highly predictable from wording patterns and product type, with text carrying more signal than product type alone. Important escalation terms include billing, fee, delinquent, disputes, and APR. Fraud and scam cases appear to behave differently and should be handled with a separate workflow rather than only a generic dispute process.

Slide 19 - Question 5: Signal Stability
For question five, we tested whether the model signal was stable enough to support product prioritization. We checked future-year performance, unseen product performance, how accuracy changed when inputs were removed, calibration of confidence scores, and sensitivity to generic complaint phrases. All five checks passed. The calibration error was 0.0018, far below the 0.08 threshold, and adding generic boilerplate barely changed predictions. The takeaway is that the signal is stable enough for decision support, while still not being causal proof.

Slide 20 - Escalation Prediction Model
This section focuses on the model itself. The model is not meant to replace human judgment. It is a prioritization tool that helps identify which complaints are more likely to represent formal escalation risk and which themes should guide product decisions.

Slide 21 - About the Model
The model converts each complaint into numeric features, learns which words and products are associated with escalation, and outputs a probability from zero to one. We evaluated it with random, temporal, and holdout-style checks. Its strengths are interpretability, speed, and scalability. Its limitation is that it relies on wording patterns, not deep language understanding or causal reasoning.

Slide 22 - Results
The model results are extremely strong in this data set. The temporal split ROC AUC is 1.0 and average precision is 1.0, while the product-only baseline ROC AUC is about 0.918. That tells us text adds major predictive value beyond product category alone. We should also be careful in how we explain this: near-perfect metrics are dataset-specific. The right interpretation is strong separability within this complaint-channel data, not a universal model that will perform perfectly everywhere.

Slide 23 - Strategic Recommendations
The recommendations turn the evidence into action. We use the complaint themes to identify what fintech PMs should build next, and we separate quick execution ideas from longer-term enterprise or strategic concepts.

Slide 24 - What Fintech PMs Should Build Next
The fintech-focused recommendations are four product moves. First is a Payment Assurance Layer: transfer status trace, expected availability timer, and exception alerts. Second is a Fraud and Scam Triage Workflow: guided intake, severity routing, freeze or claim workflow, and SLA tracking. Third is a Collections Transparency Pack for companies with lending or collections exposure: debt verification packets, communication controls, and audit trails. Fourth is Account Lifecycle Guardrails: opening, closing, payoff, and state-transition clarity. These recommendations map directly to the observed friction patterns in PayPal/Venmo and Square/Block-side rows.

Slide 25 - Conclusion
The conclusion is that CFPB complaint data can be used as a practical market-intelligence signal. The analysis shows where complaint volume concentrates, which wording patterns predict escalation, and which product areas deserve priority. The most important caveat is that this is complaint-channel evidence. It is useful for risk, product, and operations decisions, but it should be paired with customer research, business metrics, and live experiments before major investment decisions.

Slide 26 - Learnings
Our biggest learning is that customer complaints are not just negative feedback; they are structured signals about broken product journeys. We also learned that model performance is only valuable when it turns into decisions. The most useful output was not just the ROC AUC score, but the translation from model features into themes like billing clarity, dispute friction, lifecycle breaks, fraud/scam handling, and marketing expectation gaps.

Slide 27 - Q&A
Thank you. We are happy to take questions. If asked about limitations, emphasize that the data is complaint-channel specific, Chime is not explicitly named so fintech cohorts are proxies, and near-perfect model metrics are decision-support evidence rather than causal proof. If asked what to build first, recommend starting with the Payment Assurance Layer or Dispute/Fraud Fast-Lane because they are practical, measurable, and closely tied to fintech complaint patterns.
